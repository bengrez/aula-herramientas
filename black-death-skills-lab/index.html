Before you start: the story in 100 words

In 1347, ships from the Black Sea brought a deadly disease to Sicily. Within four years it had spread across almost all of Europe. People called it the Great Mortality; today we call it the Black Death. It killed somewhere between a third and a half of Europe’s people. Medieval Europe ran on the feudal system: most people were peasants who farmed land owned by a lord. Many were serfs (also called villeins), who were not free to leave the lord’s estate, the manor, and owed the lord unpaid work. Wages existed, but lords, backed by the king, expected them to stay low.

Source B · primary source

A chronicle written at Leicester Abbey, England, about 1390

Then the dreadful pestilence made its way along the coast by Southampton and reached Bristol, where almost the whole strength of the town perished, as it were surprised by sudden death… Many villages and hamlets became desolate, not a house being left in them, all having died who dwelt there.

Sheep and cattle went wandering over fields and through crops, and there was no one to go and drive or gather them, for there was such a lack of servants that no one knew what he ought to do… In the following autumn no one could get a reaper for less than 8d. with his food, or a mower for less than 12d. with his food. Therefore many crops perished in the fields for want of someone to gather them.

The king sent proclamation into all the counties that reapers and other labourers should not take more than they had been accustomed to take… But the labourers were so lifted up and obstinate that they would not listen to the king’s command, but if anyone wished to have them he had to give them what they wanted, and either lose his fruit and crops, or satisfy the lofty and covetous wishes of the workmen.

About this source. Henry Knighton was a canon (a churchman) at Leicester Abbey, a monastery that owned farmland and hired workers. He wrote his chronicle around 1390, about forty years after the plague, using the abbey’s records and people’s memories. Lifted up = proud; covetous = greedy.

Henry Knighton, Chronicle, translated from Latin in E. K. Kendall, Source-Book of English History (1900). Extracts; wording lightly simplified.

Source D · secondary source

A modern historian on what happened next

Obviously the survivors among the peasantry had the whip-hand of the lord and his bailiff. Instead of the recent hunger for land there was a shortage of men to till it… The lord of the manor could no longer cultivate his demesne land with the reduced number of serfs, while many of the strip-holdings in the open fields were thrown back on his hands, because the families that farmed them had died of plague.

About this source. G. M. Trevelyan was a British historian. He wrote this in a history of everyday life for general readers, published during the Second World War. To have the whip-hand = to be in control; to till = to farm; strip-holdings = the strips of field that peasant families farmed for themselves.

G. M. Trevelyan, English Social History (first published 1942), chapter on the Black Death.

Historian tool 1

Observation is not inference

ObservationSomething you can point to in the source. Anyone looking at it would agree it is there. “The map shows the plague reached Messina in October 1347.”
InferenceA conclusion you reach by combining the source with your own reasoning. Someone else could reach a different one. “It probably travelled by ship, because the first places hit are all ports.”
Test yourself with the sentence: I can see … so I think … because … The first blank is the observation; the rest is the inference.

Five questions to ask any source

Historian tool 3

From evidence to explanation

  1. Evidence: what the source shows. Source C shows the population fell from 4.81 to 2.60 million between 1348 and 1351.
  2. Relationship: what that connects to. Use linking words: because · so · led to · as a result · this meant that
  3. Explanation: why the link makes sense, in your own words. With almost half the people gone, every surviving worker was needed, so…
Corroboration. When two sources point the same way, say so: it makes a claim stronger. When they disagree, say how, and think about why (different time, purpose or point of view).

Reference

Glossary

The same definitions that appear when you hover over a dotted word, in alphabetical order, for the printed copy.

bailiff The lord’s manager: he ran the manor day to day, collected what was owed and made sure the serfs did their work.
biased Leaning towards one side. Every source has some bias because every author has a perspective. A biased source is still evidence, of what its author believed and wanted others to believe.
Black Death The pandemic of plague that swept across Europe, North Africa and the Middle East between 1347 and 1353. People at the time called it the Great Mortality or the pestilence; the name Black Death came centuries later. It was caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis, carried by fleas on rats and, in its lung form, passed from person to person.
canon A priest belonging to a religious community. Leicester Abbey was a large monastery that owned villages and farmland, so it was itself a landlord and an employer of workers.
cause and consequence A cause is why something happened; a consequence is what happened as a result. Historians look for chains of causes and consequences, not single reasons.
census An official count of everyone in a country. England’s first census was in 1801, so medieval populations have to be estimated.
chronicle A year-by-year record of events, usually written by a monk or churchman. Chronicles mix what the writer saw, what he was told and what he copied from other writers.
claim The statement you are arguing for. A good historical claim is clear, specific and can be tested against evidence.
corroboration When two or more sources support the same point. A corroborated claim is stronger. Sources that disagree are useful too: they show different perspectives.
demesne Said “de-MAIN”. The part of the manor the lord kept to farm for himself. Serfs had to work it as part of their duties.
d. (pence) Short for pence, from the Latin denarius. Twelve pence made a shilling. Wages like the ones Knighton records were far higher than the usual rates before the plague.
estimate A historian’s best calculation from incomplete records such as tax lists and manor records. Estimates carry uncertainty, which is why different historians give different numbers.
evidence Information from a source that you use to support a claim. Good evidence is precise: a number, a date, a quoted phrase.
feudal system The way medieval society was organised. The king granted land to lords, lords let peasants farm it, and everyone owed service to the person above them: work and produce going up, protection coming down. Historians argue about how neat this really was. The big question of this worksheet asks whether the plague weakened it.
fruit and crops Here fruit means everything the land produces, not only fruit from trees.
hamlets became desolate A hamlet is a tiny village, smaller than a village with a church. Desolate means empty and abandoned.
historian Someone who studies the past using evidence, and explains what happened and why. Historians disagree with each other, which is why you ask who wrote a source and when.
inference A conclusion you reach by combining what the source shows with your own reasoning. Use words like suggests, probably and because.
interpretation A historian’s explanation of what the evidence means. Different historians can interpret the same evidence differently.
lifted up and obstinate Proud and stubborn. Notice these words are Knighton’s judgement of the workers, not a fact he observed.
lofty and covetous Lofty here means high-and-mighty; covetous means greedy, wanting more than you should. Again, this is the writer’s opinion.
lord A nobleman, or sometimes a monastery, who owned a manor and had rights over the people living on it.
manor A lord’s estate: the village, its fields, the mill and the people on it. Each manor ran by its own customs, written down in manor court records that historians still use.
medieval From the Middle Ages: roughly the years 500 to 1500 in Europe, between the fall of Rome and the Renaissance.
observation Something you can point to in the source that anyone looking at it would agree is there.
peasant A country person who farmed the land. Most people in medieval Europe were peasants. Some were free; many were serfs.
perspective The point of view a source represents: who the author was, what they cared about, whose side they were on.
pestilence, plague An old word for a deadly epidemic disease. In these sources it always means the Black Death.
primary source Evidence created at the time of the events, or by someone who lived through them: a chronicle, a letter, a law, a painting, a tax record. Primary does not mean true. It means close to the event.
proclamation An official announcement from the king, read out in public in every county. Knighton is describing the Ordinance of Labourers (1349), which became the Statute of Labourers in 1351: a law that tried to freeze wages at their pre-plague level.
reaper, mower Harvest workers hired by the day. A reaper cut grain with a sickle; a mower cut hay with a scythe. Wages were often quoted “with his food” when meals were included.
reasoning The explanation of how your evidence supports your claim: the because that connects them.
secondary source An account written later by someone who studied the primary sources: a history book, a textbook, a documentary. Good for the big picture and for interpretation. Always ask who wrote it and when.
serf (villein) A peasant who was not free: tied to the manor, unable to leave or marry without the lord’s permission, and required to work the lord’s land some days each week without pay. The English word for a serf was villein. Serfdom faded out in England in the century after the Black Death.
servants In Knighton’s time this meant hired workers of every kind, on farms as well as in houses, not only household servants.
strip-holdings in the open fields Medieval villages farmed huge open fields divided into long strips. Each peasant family farmed scattered strips and paid the lord rent or labour for them. When a family died out, their strips went back to the lord, who then needed someone else to farm them.
till To farm the land: plough, sow and harvest.
trade routes The regular paths that merchants’ ships and caravans followed. Goods, people and diseases all travelled along them.
whip-hand To have the whip-hand is to be in control, like the person holding the reins and the whip of a horse.

Qué buscar en cada respuesta (y qué pista dar si se traba)

  • Q1 Dos hechos verificables en el mapa: llega primero a puertos del Mediterráneo (Messina oct 1347), tarda ~1 año en llegar a París/Londres, ~5 años en llegar a Moscú; Milán casi no tuvo muertes. Si dice «se propagó por barco», eso es inferencia: guardarlo para Q2.
  • Q2 Inferencia con «because»: viajó por mar y rutas de comercio porque los primeros lugares son puertos; avanzó de sur a norte; Milán sugiere que cerrar la ciudad ayudó. Pista: «¿qué tienen en común Messina, Marsella, Génova, Weymouth?».
  • Q3 Observación con números (4.81 → 2.60 millones en tres años, casi la mitad). Inferencia: casi una de cada dos personas murió; la población siguió bajando hasta 1450, o sea hubo más brotes; en 1522 aún no se recuperaba.
  • Q4 Origen: Knighton, canónigo de Leicester Abbey, c. 1390, Inglaterra. Propósito: registrar los hechos del reino para la abadía y la posteridad. Perspectiva: la Iglesia y los dueños de tierras que contratan trabajadores. Utilidad: cifras de salarios, cosechas perdidas, la orden del rey. Limitación: escrito 40 años después; no da la versión de los trabajadores; sólo Inglaterra.
  • Q5 Sesgo en las palabras («lifted up and obstinate», «lofty and covetous wishes») = mirada del empleador. Útil igual: los números (8d., 12d.), la orden del rey y las cosechas perdidas son hechos comprobables, y el sesgo mismo muestra cómo pensaban los señores. Pista: «busca las palabras que juzgan a los trabajadores; luego busca los números».
  • Q6 D puede: ver el largo plazo, comparar muchas fuentes, explicar consecuencias. B puede: dar detalle de testigo, cifras de la época, el tono de cómo se vivía. Cualquier par razonable vale.
  • Q7 Eslabones esperables: (1) campos, cosechas y animales quedan sin quien los atienda (B); (2) los señores siguen necesitando que se trabaje su tierra y compiten por los pocos trabajadores (B, D); (3) el rey intenta congelar salarios y los trabajadores no obedecen; el señor no puede cultivar su demesne con menos siervos (B, D). Pista: «¿qué pasó con las cosechas según Knighton? ¿qué necesitaba el señor?».
  • Q8 Vale cualquier elección bien justificada: C para «mueren muchos» (números de historiadores); B para salarios (testigo, cifras concretas); D para el cambio feudal (explicación de historiador). Lo que importa es que diga por qué es fuerte (fiable, precisa, cercana al hecho, corroborada).
  • Q9 Sí, se corroboran: C da la escala de la pérdida; D explica su efecto sobre señores y siervos. Son tipos distintos (datos vs interpretación) pero apuntan igual. Respuesta fuerte: cita un número de C y una frase de D.
  • Q10 Una respuesta fuerte: fija una posición («partly» o «strongly» ambas defendibles), usa dos fuentes o más, y conecta la evidencia con «el sistema feudal se debilitó» (los siervos ganan poder de negociación, el demesne no se puede trabajar, la ley de salarios del rey es ignorada). Bonus si pone un matiz: los señores resistieron (la orden del rey) y el cambio tardó décadas.
