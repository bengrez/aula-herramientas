Atoms, the table, and why things stick together

Everything on this page comes from one idea: an atom's outer electrons decide what it does. Where it sits on the periodic table, how strongly it pulls on electrons, what it bonds to and how — all of it follows from that. Work through it in order; each part is built on the one before.

Where this sits in your course. Your Term 1 Chemistry unit covers elements and atoms, the periodic table, metals and non-metals, and physical versus chemical change. So Parts 1 to 4 of this page are your actual coursework, not extra. Part 5 — bonding and electronegativity — runs ahead of it, and is normally Year 9–10. Nothing there is assumed knowledge; it is built from scratch on the parts before it.

Part 1 — Review

What an atom is made of

An atom has a tiny, dense nucleus with almost all the mass, and electrons around it holding almost all the volume. If the nucleus were a marble on the centre circle of a football oval, the nearest electrons would be out past the boundary fence. Atoms are mostly empty space.

Two numbers to keep straight

Atomic number (Z) = number of protons. It is the element's identity and it is the number the periodic table is ordered by.
Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons. So neutrons = A − Z.
In a neutral atom, electrons = protons. An atom that has gained or lost electrons is an ion, and it is no longer neutral.

How the models got better

Each model kept what worked and fixed what did not. That is how science normally moves — not one genius getting it right, but each person finding the specific thing the last model could not explain.

The one that matters

Electrons in the outer shell are called valence electrons. Atoms react in order to end up with a full outer shell — 8 electrons for most of the elements you will meet, or 2 for the very small ones. Every bond in Part 5 is an atom solving that problem.

Part 2 — Review

Reading the periodic table

Columns = groups

Elements in the same column have the same number of valence electrons, so they behave alike. Group 1 all have 1 outer electron and all react violently with water. Group 18 all have a full outer shell and essentially do not react at all.

Rows = periods

Elements in the same row have the same number of shells. Going across a row, you add one proton and one electron each step, filling the same outer shell until it is full — then a new row starts.

Part 3 — Review

Why the properties trend the way they do

Two forces are competing for every outer electron: the pull of the protons in the nucleus, and the shielding of all the inner electrons in between. Every trend on the table falls out of those two.

Going across → (left to right)

Protons increase, but electrons are being added to the same shell, so shielding barely changes. Net effect: the nucleus pulls harder.

  • Atomic radius decreases
  • Electronegativity increases
  • Metallic character decreases

Going down ↓ (top to bottom)

Each step adds a whole new shell. The outer electrons are further out and shielded by everything underneath. Net effect: the nucleus pulls more weakly.

  • Atomic radius increases
  • Electronegativity decreases
  • Metallic character increases
Electronegativity, in one sentence

Electronegativity is how strongly an atom pulls on the electrons in a bond. It runs on the Pauling scale from caesium at 0.79 to fluorine at 3.98 — fluorine is the greediest atom there is. Everything in Part 5 is a tug-of-war decided by the difference between two of these numbers.

Common mix-up

Electronegativity is not the same as reactivity. Fluorine is the most electronegative element, but caesium is far more reactive — it is desperate to give away its outer electron, which is the opposite behaviour. The most reactive metals sit bottom-left; the most reactive non-metals sit top-right.

Switch the table in Part 2 to Electronegativity and look at it as a landscape: it climbs towards the top-right corner and falls away to the bottom-left. Noble gases are left blank — they generally do not form bonds, so a bond-pulling number would be meaningless for them.

The staircase is a convention, not a cliff

Element properties change gradually, so the boundary is drawn where it is useful rather than where nature puts a wall. Almost everyone agrees on the six metalloids listed above; polonium and astatine sit right on the line and different textbooks classify them differently. If a source disagrees with another about those two, neither is wrong — the category is ours, not nature's.

Where this meets the rest of your unit

The same unit works with elements, compounds and mixtures. Everything above is what sits underneath that distinction:

  • An element is one kind of atom — one box on the table. Fe. O.
  • A compound is two or more elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio. That bond is exactly what Part 5 is about. H2O. NaCl.
  • A mixture is substances together but not bonded, in any ratio, and separable physically. Air. Seawater.

So the test for compound versus mixture is: is there a chemical bond? Which is why bonding is worth understanding early.

Part 5 — New

Why atoms bond, and which bond you get

Atoms bond for one reason: a full outer shell is a lower-energy, more stable arrangement than an incomplete one. There are three ways to get there, and which one happens depends on how hard each atom pulls on the electrons.

Ionic — one atom takes

A metal has a weak grip on its outer electrons; a non-metal pulls hard. The electron is transferred. Both atoms become ions with full shells, and the opposite charges attract.

Na + Cl: sodium gives up its one outer electron and becomes Na+. Chlorine takes it, completing its shell, and becomes Cl−. The attraction between them is the bond.

Covalent — both atoms share

Two non-metals both pull hard, so neither can take. They share a pair of electrons, and the shared pair counts towards both atoms' outer shells.

H + Cl: each contributes one electron to a shared pair. If the two atoms pull equally the sharing is even (non-polar); if one pulls harder the pair sits closer to it (polar).

Metallic — everyone shares everything

In a metal, every atom releases its outer electrons into a shared pool. You get a lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons. Those free-moving electrons are the reason metals conduct electricity, and the reason a metal can be hammered flat without shattering — the ions slide past each other and the electron sea just flows around them.

The electronegativity rule

Take the two electronegativities and subtract the smaller from the larger. Call it ΔEN ("delta EN"). It tells you how one-sided the tug-of-war is.

The rule is a guide, not a law

ΔEN alone misclassifies real compounds. HF has ΔEN = 1.78, which the table above would call ionic — but HF is a polar covalent molecule, because two non-metals cannot form an ionic bond no matter what the numbers say. So always ask both questions: what does ΔEN say, and what kinds of element are these? When they disagree, the element types win. The predictor below applies both.
